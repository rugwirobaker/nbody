# RFC-002: Contact-based merging

**Status:** specified and characterised, not yet implemented
**Amends:** [RFC-001](RFC-001.md) §2.6 (the merge trigger) and Step 10's threshold clause
**Language:** Zig · **Depends on:** RFC-001, which remains in force everywhere this document is silent

---

## 0. Why

RFC-001 §2.6 fires a merge when two particles come within a fixed distance:
$d^2 < d_{merge}^2$, one threshold for every pair regardless of mass. That rule
is sound and it is what Parts 2 and 3 implement.

It cannot be drawn. A renderer showing bodies at all must pick a size for them,
and under a fixed threshold that size means nothing — the drawn radius and the
merge distance are unrelated numbers. In `nbody-viz` the gap is large and grows
with mass:

| body | drawn radius | discs touch at | merges at | overstated by |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| mass 1 | 0.008 | 0.016 | 0.00224 | 7× |
| mass 25 | 0.040 | 0.080 | 0.00224 | 36× |
| mass 94 | 0.078 | 0.155 | 0.00224 | 69× |

At the demo's default zoom a mass-94 body is drawn 21 px across and merges only
when another body's centre comes within 0.6 px of its own. Two large bodies can
sit fully overlapped on screen and remain dozens of times too far apart to
merge, and when the merge does fire the two centres were already sub-pixel, so
nothing visible happens. A viewer cannot predict a merge, and the picture
actively misleads about which bodies are interacting.

This RFC replaces the trigger with a contact test, so that a body's drawn size
*is* its merge cross-section.

---

## 1. The rule (normative)

### 1.1 Body radius

Give every particle a radius derived from its mass. A disc of radius $r$ has
area $\pi r^2$; at constant density $\rho$, mass is proportional to area, so
$m = \rho \pi r^2$ and

$$r(m) = k\sqrt{m}, \qquad k = \frac{1}{\sqrt{\pi\rho}}$$

$k$ is therefore a density constant, and in a simulation with arbitrary units
it is a single number chosen once. It is the 2D counterpart of $r \propto
m^{1/3}$ for spheres.

Radius is **derived, never stored** — a method on the particle, not a field:

```zig
/// A body's radius (RFC-002 §1.1). Derived from mass, never stored.
pub fn radius(p: Particle, cfg: Config) f32 {
    return cfg.merge_radius_scale * @sqrt(p.mass);
}
```

This is the single definition of a body's size. The merge rule, the tests, and
the renderer all go through it, so no two of them can disagree about how big a
body is.

Mass determines radius completely. Storing it would create a second copy of what
the particle already holds, and every write to `mass` would become a write that
must also update `radius` — seeding and merging today, and whatever comes later.
Missing one yields a body that merges at the wrong distance: a plausible-looking
simulation, in a demo-only path, that no conservation test would catch. In the
SoA build a derived radius also stays out of the padding invariant (RFC-001
§3.2), leaving one fewer array whose slots past `n` must be maintained.

A seventh field would be cheap, which is worth recording so the question is not
reopened looking for a hidden win. It widens the AoS stride from 24 bytes to 28;
the same Phase A loop over the same particles, at three struct sizes:

```
        n            24 B            28 B            32 B    28 vs 24    32 vs 24
     1024         1140954         1172088         1157575        2.7%        1.5%
     2048         4613183         4689317         4627204        1.7%        0.3%
     4096        18604806        18745597        18569056        0.8%       -0.2%
     8192        75365000        76315125        74116819        1.3%       -1.7%
```

0.8–2.7 %, and padding to 32 bytes is sometimes faster than 24. Phase A is
limited by how fast the arithmetic issues rather than by how fast memory
arrives — RFC-001 §2.5's plateau works out to 4.4 cycles for fourteen float
operations per pair — so 17 % more bytes per particle costs almost nothing, and
none of it would move the speedup figure past its quoted precision.

This holds while mass is the only thing radius depends on. A body whose size
also depends on its history — a burnt-out remnant against a star of the same
mass — would carry that history as stored state, and the argument above would
have to be made again on the new terms.

### 1.2 The trigger

Two bodies merge when their discs overlap — when the distance between their
centres falls below the sum of their radii:

$$d^2 < \big(r(m_i) + r(m_j)\big)^2 = k^2\big(\sqrt{m_i} + \sqrt{m_j}\big)^2$$

This replaces §2.6's $d^2 < d_{merge}^2$. Squared on both sides, as before: the
test is a comparison, and no square root of $d$ is taken.

### 1.3 The constant

$$k = 5.0 \times 10^{-4}$$

for the seeded disk of RFC-001 §2.7 at its default mass range and radius 1. §2
records how that was measured. Implementations expose it as
`merge_radius_scale` in `Config`, replacing `d_merge2`.

## 2. Choosing k

$k$ sets the accretion rate, and the rate is unforgiving in both directions: too
small and nothing ever merges, too large and the disk eats itself before it has
orbited once. It was measured rather than derived, over 16,000 ticks at
$n = 1000$, `dt` = 2.5e-4, seed `0xC0FFEE`, disk preset:

```
   k        mass-1 pair    tick 4000              tick 16000
              contacts     n     largest  E/E0     n   largest  E/E0
  0.0005      0.00100     556      2.7%  0.975    145    11.3%  0.865
  0.0010      0.00200     386      2.4%  0.908     81    13.8%  0.491
  0.0020      0.00400     260      6.1%  0.692     35    16.6%  0.283
  0.0040      0.00800     160      7.8%  0.566     18    16.5%  0.377
  0.0080      0.01600      94      8.3%  0.592     10    27.5%  0.248
```

`E/E0` is total energy against its value at $t = 0$. It moves because merging
leaks energy upward and radiative cooling drains it downward, and §7.2 measures
both — the two are large and nearly cancel, so read this column as corroboration
and the survivor counts as the evidence. `largest` is the biggest body's share
of total mass.

**$k = 5\times10^{-4}$ is gentler than the fixed rule it replaces.** The
demo currently ships $d_{merge} = 2.24\times10^{-3}$ and reaches tick 4000 with
306 survivors and `E/E0` = 0.895. Contact merging at this $k$ reaches the same
tick with 556 survivors and 0.975. The improvement comes from where the rule
spends its cross-section: a fixed threshold treats a speck and a planet alike,
while the contact rule gives dust a small target and reserves the large
cross-sections for bodies that have earned them.

**Above $5\times10^{-4}$ the run degrades quickly.** By $k = 10^{-3}$ energy is
off by half at tick 16,000. The cause is the rule's own feedback: cross-section
grows with mass, so every merge makes the next one likelier, and the tail of a
run is far more violent than its head.

**Runaway was looked for and not found.** The largest body's share climbs
0.5 % → 2.7 % → 6.5 % → 11.3 % across successive doublings of elapsed ticks —
steady growth, not divergence. §7.1 follows it out to twelve orbits, where the
population settles and stops changing.

`dt` is unchanged at 2.5e-4 for the demo. The energy figures above were measured
at that resolution and hold.

## 3. Softening (supersedes §2.6's rationale)

RFC-001 §2.6 justifies its default this way: *"Default $d_{merge} \approx
\varepsilon$: the simulation then never lingers in the regime where softening
distorts the force law — the two mechanisms cover for each other."*

That rationale does not survive contact merging, and it had already stopped
holding before this RFC. With $\varepsilon = 0.0224$ and $k = 5\times10^{-4}$, a
pair of mass-1 bodies contacts at $d = 0.001$, which is $0.045\,\varepsilon$ —
deep inside the softened regime. It takes a pair of mass-500 bodies to contact
at $\varepsilon$.

**This is accepted, deliberately.** Two reasons:

1. **Softening bounds the encounter.** $\varepsilon$ flattens the force at short
   range, which caps the acceleration and keeps the integrator stable. A pair
   approaching inside it feels a weaker attraction than a true inverse square
   would give, so the encounter is gentler. §2 measures the outcome directly:
   energy holds to 2.5 % at tick 4000.

2. **§2.6's default was already abandoned.** `nbody-viz` runs at
   $d_{merge} = \varepsilon/10$, because the $\varepsilon$-scale default merges
   85 % of the population within the first simulated second and drives total
   energy from −992 to positive, unbinding the system. The principle lost an
   argument with measurement some time ago; this RFC records that rather than
   continuing to contradict it silently.

The rejected alternative was flooring the rule at
$\max(\varepsilon,\, r_i + r_j)$, which preserves §2.6's intent. It is rejected
because it puts a seam in the rule exactly where the rule is supposed to be
visible: below the floor, bodies would merge without touching, and the picture
would go back to lying — in the small, where this change is meant to stop it.

## 4. What this does not change (normative)

The trigger is the whole of the change. Everything below stays exactly as
RFC-001 specifies, and an implementation that alters any of it is
non-conforming:

- **Every one of Step 10's merge formulas.** Mass additive; velocity and
  position mass-weighted; `heat` accumulating both parents' heat plus
  $\Delta KE = \tfrac12 \mu v_{rel}^2$. What a merge *does* is untouched; only
  *when* it fires moves.
- **The energy ledger and its known leak.** The merged pair's mutual potential
  still departs with the pair, so `KE + PE + Σheat` still steps up at each
  merge. §7.2 measures the new size: a median of 0.019 % of $|E_0|$ per merge
  against the fixed rule's 0.018 %, with a higher 90th percentile and a lower
  maximum. Existence and sign are unchanged.
- **Phase A, in full.** Same softened force law, same ordered-pair $n^2$
  traversal, same frozen snapshot (Step 8), same prohibition on fusing phases.
- **Phase C's placement and mechanics.** After integration, never inside Phase
  A; greedy chain handling in fixed scan order with restart; swap-remove;
  re-zeroing the vacated slot to preserve the padding invariant (RFC-001 §3.2).
- **The measurement rules.** RFC-001 §2.5 in full — `ReleaseFast`,
  `merging = false` for every benchmark, Phase-A ns/tick, never FPS. Merging is
  off in every measured run, so this change moves no published number.
- **The two-implementation rule.** Scalar and SIMD implement the same algorithm.
  Contact merging lands in both or in neither.

## 5. Implementation notes

### 5.1 Radii are precomputed once per pass

The naive reading of §1.2 evaluates two square roots per candidate pair, which
would put a `sqrt` inside a scan that RFC-001 §2.6 is careful to keep free of
one. Compute the radii once into a scratch array at the top of each Phase C
pass:

```
r[i] = k * sqrt(mass[i])     for i in 0..n        — O(n)
merge i,j when d² < (r[i] + r[j])²                 — squared compare, no sqrt
```

The greedy rule restarts the scan after every merge (§2.6), and a merge changes
one mass, so the array is recomputed per pass. That is $O(n)$ against the scan's
$O(n^2)$, and it leaves §2.6's vectorization note intact: the inner test is
still a squared comparison against a value that is now loaded rather than
constant.

**The array belongs to `Sim` and `Particles`**, alongside `ax`/`ay`. It is
scratch rather than state: rebuilt from `mass` at the top of every pass, so it
has no opportunity to disagree with the masses it came from. Reusing `ax` — dead
by Phase C, and therefore free — was considered and rejected, because one array
holding two unrelated things breaks RFC-001 §3.2's expectation about what
`ax`/`ay` contain past `n`.

Three formulations were measured, one full $O(n^2)$ pass, no merges performed:

```
       n           fixed    sqrt per pair       algebraic     precomputed
    1000          193542           387542          401750          243833
    4000         2987291          6221583         6391000         3838083
```

Against the fixed threshold this replaces, at $n = 4000$: **precomputed 1.28×,
square root per pair 2.08×, algebraic 2.14×.** The two per-pair forms are within
3 % of each other — tied, within noise — because both do their work once per
pair, and pairs outnumber particles by thousands to one. Precomputing moves that
work to once per particle, which is the whole of the difference.

"Algebraic" is the sign-checked double squaring: with
$B = d^2 - k^2(m_i + m_j)$, merge when $B < 0$ or $B^2 < 4k^4 m_i m_j$. The case
split is unavoidable, since squaring an inequality only preserves its direction
when both sides are non-negative and $B$ can be either. It removes the square
root and buys nothing. Recorded here so it is not proposed again as an
optimisation.

**Adding the array does not perturb Phase A.** Allocating it shifts every
allocation after it to a different address, which was tested directly by padding
`Sim`'s allocations four ways:

```
        n    kernel         shift 0    shift 1    shift 2    shift 3
     1024    scalar         1157083    1156035    1172633    1156631   spread 1.4%
     1024      simd          396748     396727     396802     396685   spread 0.0%
     4096    scalar        18463217   18498533   18889683   18630367   spread 2.3%
     4096      simd         6372900    6570600    6385342    6363075   spread 3.3%
```

Everything sits inside RFC-001 §2.5's stated run-to-run variation, and the SIMD
row at $n = 1024$ repeats to within 0.03 %. Phase A walks its arrays front to
back in order, and that access pattern does not care where the arrays start.

### 5.2 Padding is untouched

The SoA scan iterates `0..p.n` on both indices, so slots at index ≥ `n` never
enter the test. Zero-mass padding would otherwise be a hazard here: a padding
slot has $r = k\sqrt{0} = 0$, and a live body passing within its own radius of a
padding slot's position would satisfy the contact test against a ghost. The
existing scan bounds prevent this, and implementations **must not** widen them.

### 5.3 Configuration

`Config.d_merge2` is replaced by `Config.merge_radius_scale`, defaulting to
$5\times10^{-4}$. `nbody-viz` drops its `demo_d_merge2` override, since the
measured constant is now the library default for this disk.

`Config.validate` **must** reject a negative or non-finite scale. A scale of
exactly zero is legal and disables merging geometrically, which is a different
mechanism from `merging = false` and is not a substitute for it.

### 5.4 Acceptance tests

RFC-001 §2.5's merge tests carry over with their triggers re-expressed: they
assert momentum, total mass, centre-of-mass drift, determinism, and the
single-merge energy ledger, none of which depend on what fired the merge. Tests
that place two particles at a chosen separation to force a merge now compute
that separation from the masses they assign.

One new test is required: **two bodies of unequal mass merge when their discs
touch and not before**. Place them at $r_i + r_j + \delta$ and assert no merge;
place them at $r_i + r_j - \delta$ and assert exactly one. This is the property
the whole RFC exists to establish, and it is the one a fixed threshold cannot
express.

## 6. Rendering (non-normative)

The point of the change is that the renderer can now draw the truth: a body's
disc is its merge cross-section, and two discs touching means a merge this tick.
`nbody-viz` draws at §1.1's `radius()` — the same function the merge rule calls,
reached by exporting `merge_radius_scale` from the wasm module rather than
copying the constant into JavaScript. A renderer with its own copy of $k$ is a
renderer that can drift out of agreement with the physics, which is the exact
failure this RFC exists to end.

Two consequences follow, and neither is optional in practice.

**The picture gets much smaller.** $k = 5\times10^{-4}$ is 16× below the
cosmetic radius the demo draws with today:

| | drawn today | at $r(m)$, default zoom |
| --- | --- | --- |
| mass 1 | 2.2 px | 0.14 px |
| mass 10 | 6.9 px | 0.43 px |
| mass 100 | 21.8 px | 1.4 px |

**A display floor is therefore needed**, clamping the drawn radius to roughly a
pixel so that dust remains visible. Bodies below the floor are drawn larger than
they are, which reintroduces the overstatement this RFC removes — but only among
particles whose true size is sub-pixel, where no honest rendering exists and
where the viewer has nothing to predict. Above the floor the drawn disc is
exact, which covers the large orbiting pairs that motivated the change.

**Zoom range should widen** so a merge can be watched at the scale it happens.
The reference implementation this project follows takes the same three steps —
`radius = 0.45·√m`, a `clamp(0.7, 50.0)` on displayed radius, and a mouse
wheel — and its particles are about a pixel across at default zoom.

## 7. Measured behaviour

### 7.1 The disk converges

One orbit at the disk edge is 35,386 ticks at the demo's `dt`, so §2's sweep
covered under half an orbit. Run out to twelve, at $k = 5\times10^{-4}$:

```
   orbits      tick       n   largest      E/E0
        0         0    1000      0.1%     1.000
        1     35386      96     11.4%     0.784
        2     70772      73     12.3%     0.866
        3    106157      66     18.7%     0.620
        5    176928      59     18.7%     0.600
        7    247700      59     18.7%     0.600
        8    283085      58     26.2%     0.473
       11    389242      57     26.2%     0.518
```

**The population converges to 57–60 bodies and stays there.** 943 merges happen
across twelve orbits and the overwhelming majority land in the first, when the
disk is still dense. After that the survivors are far enough apart that
encounters are rare, and the system is effectively stable.

The largest body's mass share is a **staircase, not a curve**: it holds at
18.7 % for orbits 3 through 7, steps to 26.2 % at orbit 8, and holds again
through orbit 11. Each step is a single merge between two of the few remaining
large bodies. There is no runaway — the rule's feedback (cross-section growing
with mass) never gets going, because it runs out of material first.

### 7.2 The energy books, and why `E/E0` is a weak signal

The energy step a merge causes is the change in potential: the pair's own term
departs with the pair, and every external term moves because the mass relocates
to the centre of mass. The kinetic term cancels exactly against `heat`, which
Step 10 credits with precisely the kinetic energy it destroys. Measured per
merge, as a share of $|E_0|$, over 8,000 ticks:

```
                          merges      p50       p90       max
  contact k = 5e-4           735    0.019%    0.232%    4.496%
  fixed d_merge2 = 5e-6      787    0.018%    0.141%    9.258%
```

The distribution is heavy-tailed: the median merge is two orders of magnitude
cheaper than the worst one. RFC-001 Step 10's "around 0.3 % of total energy per
merge" is a fair description of the tail and a poor one of the typical case, and
a single figure should not be quoted for it. Contact merging has a **higher**
90th percentile and a **lower** maximum than the fixed rule — it fires more
often at moderate separations and less often at the violent ones.

Summed, those steps are large, and the full accounting explains where they go:

```
                          merge PE    radiated    residual       net
  contact k = 5e-4          +89.3%      -80.5%      -0.08%     +8.7%
  fixed d_merge2 = 5e-6     +71.0%      -79.6%      -0.04%     -8.6%
```

**Merging pumps energy in and `heat_decay` takes it back out.** Radiative
cooling deletes heat from the ledger every tick — RFC-001 calls it presentation
rather than physics, and this is what that costs — and over a run it very nearly
offsets the merge leak. The residual is the integrator, and at 0.08 % of $|E_0|$
over 8,000 ticks it is negligible; semi-implicit Euler is doing its job. At a
timestep four times finer the two large terms grow together (+96.0 % and
−96.8 %) and the residual falls further, to 0.02 %.

Three consequences:

1. **`E/E0` is a difference of two large opposing terms**, and its value is
   therefore a weak indicator of anything. §2 selected $k$ partly on it; that
   selection stands on the survivor counts and mass shares, which are robust,
   and the energy column there should be read as corroboration rather than as
   evidence.
2. **The near-cancellation is a coincidence, not a mechanism.** It holds at the
   demo's `heat_decay`; change that constant and the two terms stop matching.
   Nothing should be built on it.
3. **None of this is new.** The fixed rule shipping today behaves the same way,
   so this RFC neither causes the effect nor fixes it. Closing the ledger
   honestly — banking the vanished potential, and accounting radiated heat
   rather than deleting it — is a separate change to RFC-001 Step 10, and a
   candidate for a future RFC.

### 7.3 `keplerian` needs no special handling

The central body has mass 50 (`central_mass_factor` 50 × nominal mean mass 1),
giving it a radius of 0.00354 under §1.1, so a mass-1 body contacts it at
0.00404 — a small target at the origin, which most of the disk orbits well
outside.

```
    tick       n    merges    central     share       E/E0
       0    1000         0       50.0      4.7%     1.000
    4000     541       459       50.0      4.7%     0.957
    8000     288       712      100.2      9.5%     0.877
   12000     207       793      115.9     11.0%     0.868
   16000     162       838      117.4     11.1%     0.908
```

The central body accretes to about 2.3× its seeded mass over 16,000 ticks and
settles at 11 % of total mass — growth, not domination. Survivor counts track
the `disk` preset closely (162 against 145) and energy holds slightly better.
The constant chosen in §2 carries over, and no preset-specific value is
needed.
